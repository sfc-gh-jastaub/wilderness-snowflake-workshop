The brief

Wilderness Outfitters is a big-box Australian outdoor retailer: 72 stores across every state and the ACT, plus online, selling camping, hiking, fishing, kayaking and snow gear, with about 180,000 Basecamp loyalty members. In Australia, Christmas is summer. The eight weeks from 1 November to Christmas Eve are camping season and bring in about a quarter of the year's sales.

Last Christmas Wilderness sold out of tents in the second week of December, so this year the buyers ordered tents 40% deeper. Sleeping bags, mats, stoves and lights were ordered as usual.

It is Wednesday 4 November 2026. The Christmas catalogue dropped on Sunday, and the first weekend was the biggest November weekend Wilderness has had.

"Everyone wants a tent for Christmas, and we bet on it. Three days in, the bet looks right in Sydney and Brisbane and wrong in Melbourne and Hobart. Every family that buys a tent also needs somewhere to sleep and something to cook on, and I don't think we bought enough of either. And the Summit 2P on our catalogue cover is already coming back with broken poles. I have seven weeks. My store managers need to know every morning what to sell, what to move and what to send back, and today the answers sit in three systems, a stack of supplier PDFs and twenty thousand reviews nobody reads." How it works today

Each of Martha's three questions has an answer somewhere. None of them reaches a store manager by opening time.

  • What to sell. Sales come from the tills and the website into the sales system overnight. Head office sends a weekly sales report on a Monday, which reaches stores after the weekend it describes.
  • What to move. Stock on hand and on order sit in the merchandising system, by store and by batch. Seeing which stores will run out and which will be left holding stock means exporting both systems into a spreadsheet and matching them by hand.
  • What to send back. The returns desk records a reason code on each return, and "faulty" covers everything from a snapped pole to a customer who could not pitch the tent. What the customer actually said is in a free-text note nobody reads.
  • The fine print. What suppliers will take back, and by when, is in their agreements, which arrive as PDFs. The buying guides that tell staff what goes with a tent are PDFs too.
  • The customers. Reviews, returns notes and post-purchase surveys add up to twenty thousand comments. They are where the broken Summit 2P poles showed up first.

It takes a merchandising analyst most of a day to pull that together for one question in one state. Martha needs it for every store, every morning, until Christmas Eve.
